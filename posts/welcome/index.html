Pangolins, pathogens, and the price of land-use change

Why Southeast Asia’s wildlife trade and shifting landscapes keep putting people, livestock, and wild animals on the same map — and what One Health thinking might do about it.

Welcome — thanks for stopping by. I wanted the first post here to be about something I keep thinking about as a vet, a wildlife disease ecologist, and someone who grew up in Southeast Asia: the places where people, wild animals, and pathogens are pushed closer together, and how often that push is something we do to ourselves.

The pangolin on the cover

The most-trafficked wild mammal on Earth is almost certainly a pangolin. All eight species — four in Africa, four in Asia, including the Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica) across the region I come from — are listed on CITES Appendix I, and all eight are in some shade of red on the IUCN list. Their scales, made of the same keratin as my fingernails, are smuggled by the tonne into traditional-medicine markets. Their meat is a luxury dish. Estimates from seizure data suggest more than a million pangolins have been pulled out of the wild over the last two decades, and every estimate people bother to make turns out to be an undercount.

That number matters beyond the animals themselves. Pangolins became household names in 2020 when their role in the early SARS-CoV-2 investigation put them briefly on every front page. I don’t think pangolins caused that pandemic. But the episode made something visible that wildlife veterinarians had been quietly writing about for years: illegally traded wildlife is a moving buffet of pathogens, carried through live-animal markets, back-road holding pens, airport baggage, and courier networks that no diagnostic lab has standing access to.

Tracking wildlife in Southeast Asia

Part of why this is so hard to manage is that we have a very uneven picture of what’s moving around. My own work has been on the resident side of that picture — GPS collars on gaur and banteng, habitat-suitability models across thirteen Asian countries, stochastic disease models that ask what happens if a cattle-borne pathogen actually meets a wild bovid population.

A couple of things you learn quickly from that kind of tracking work:

  • Wild bovid habitat in Southeast Asia is fragmented and squeezed up against the working landscape. Over half of the habitat that is still ecologically suitable for gaur, banteng, and wild water buffalo sits outside protected areas. That isn’t a failure of conservation; it’s a description of a continent where national parks are islands in a sea of agriculture, plantation, and human settlement.
  • Where wild bovid habitat and domestic cattle density overlap, you get interface zones — forest edges, cropland borders, shared water points — that are exactly the kinds of places where pathogens like foot-and-mouth disease, haemorrhagic septicaemia, or brucellosis can move in either direction.
  • Illegal hunting, snaring, and wildlife trafficking cut right through these same zones. So the same corridors that matter for conservation are also the corridors that matter for pathogen movement and for the wildlife trade.

It is, in other words, one landscape with several problems stacked on top of each other.

Land-use change is a disease driver

Most of the big emerging infectious disease events of the last fifty years — Nipah, SARS, MERS, Ebola, highly pathogenic avian influenza, monkeypox/mpox, SARS-CoV-2 — share a pattern. A pathogen that was quietly circulating in a wild host encountered humans (or human-adjacent animals like pigs, poultry, civets, or camels) because the edge between wild and not-wild shifted. A forest was cleared, a plantation was expanded, a cave was mined, a live-animal market scaled up, a trade route opened.

The published evidence on this has become hard to argue with. Ecological studies show that deforestation and landscape fragmentation correlate with increases in zoonotic disease spillover events, because they change which animals live where, which pathogens they carry, and how often any of that touches a human community. The wildlife trade amplifies the effect — it takes that edge and moves it, physically, into airports and cities.

The implication for my field is uncomfortable and, I think, freeing: surveillance designed around known pathogens and known hosts will always be chasing the last outbreak. Surveillance designed around the interfaces — forest edges being cleared, markets that handle wild species, transport chains that aggregate animals — has a chance of catching the next one.

One Health, honestly

“One Health” gets used so often in grant applications that it can start to sound like a polite slogan. I want to try to keep it concrete on this blog. For me, One Health means at least three things:

  • Shared data and shared surveillance. Human, animal, and environmental health agencies in most of Southeast Asia (and most of the world) still track their own subsets of the picture in their own systems. The useful work is often the boring work of getting those datasets to speak to each other.
  • Conservation as disease prevention. Keeping forests standing, keeping wild populations viable, and enforcing wildlife-trade regulations aren’t separate from pandemic preparedness — they are pandemic preparedness, delivered upstream. The pangolin that stays in the forest doesn’t enter a supply chain.
  • Field-first, lab-second. The most important questions are almost never answered by a sample alone. They’re answered by someone who has actually stood in the farm, the forest edge, or the market, and can tell you what “normal” looks like there.
